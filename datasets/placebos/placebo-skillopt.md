# Notes on judgement

Most of what makes a person good at deciding never gets said out loud. It shows
up as a habit of attention, a tolerance for staying unsure a little longer than
is comfortable, a willingness to be the one who says the plain thing in a room
that would rather not hear it. None of that has the shape of a method. It is
closer to a temperament, and a temperament can be worked on, slowly, mostly by
watching what your own does when nobody else is present.

What follows is about that texture. The conditions a judgement gets made in,
the pressures acting on it, and the ways a person can be wrong about their own
thinking without catching themselves at it.

## The company a question keeps

A choice never arrives on its own. It lands in a week that already has a shape,
among other things being weighed, other people waiting, other obligations that
take up room regardless. Written down, the question looks like a self-contained
object. It never was one.

This matters because attention runs out, which people find hard to believe
about themselves. The same person, given the same question on a quiet morning
and at the end of a long stretch of small demands, will produce two different
answers and hold both with the same confidence. Neither is
careless. The second one is simply made with less of the person available to
make it.

Anyone who takes their own judgement seriously eventually asks where in the
week it is being formed, and treats the answer as evidence about how far to
trust it. That question costs nothing and gets asked almost never, since the
state you are in is the one thing invisible from inside it.

## Weather

Mood is the least respectable influence on thinking and among the strongest.
Sleep, hunger, the hour, an unrelated conversation that went badly an hour
before: all of it colours what looks risky and what looks obvious, and none of
it announces itself as an influence. It presents as the world having changed.

Feeling is worth keeping hold of. It carries real information about what a
person cares about, and that information is often the most reliable thing
available. What is worth separating is the part that belongs to
the question from the part that belongs to the day. Something that looks
frightening at night and manageable at noon has not moved. You have.

The practical version is unglamorous and works. Notice when a judgement formed
in bad weather and give it a second look in better weather. Sometimes the
second look confirms the first exactly, which is worth knowing too.

## Borrowed conviction

Certainty travels between people far more easily than the reasons behind it. A
person who speaks firmly hands over the firmness whole, and the grounds stay
behind with them. Some days later the borrowed confidence has settled in and
feels indistinguishable from a view you arrived at yourself, and you can no
longer say where it came from or what it rests on.

Some people speak with force about everything, including subjects they took up
an hour ago. Others hedge everything, including subjects they have lived inside
for years. Manner tells you little about knowledge, and the forceful manner
spreads.

The useful question is what the confident person knows that you do not. It is
asked kindly and it is answerable. Sometimes there is a real body of experience
behind the tone and it is worth taking seriously. Sometimes the answer is that
they talk that way about everything, and once that is clear their certainty
stops being a reason.

## The pull of a well-told story

An account that hangs together persuades more than one that does not, and how
well an account hangs together is a poor guide to whether it is true.
Coherence can be manufactured. It is what happens when the parts that did not
fit have been quietly left out, and the leaving out is rarely deliberate. Memory
does it on its own, smoothing as it goes.

This applies to accounts other people give and, more awkwardly, to your own. A
situation you can describe cleanly, each element leading to the next, has
usually been through some editing. The rough description, loose ends still
hanging off it and the contradictions left in, is less convincing and closer to
the truth.

Fluency is worth being suspicious of in proportion to how good it feels. When
an explanation arrives easily and completely, that ease is a fact about the
explanation, not about the world it claims to describe.

## Reasons that arrive afterwards

People generally settle on something and then produce the reasoning for it. The
reasoning is sincere. It is believed by the person offering it, and it
frequently has little to do with whatever actually moved them. Introspection
does not report the process. It reports a tidied reconstruction, assembled
after the fact and shaped to be presentable.

The consequence is worth sitting with. Your own stated reason for a preference
is evidence about that preference, but weak evidence. When someone dismantles
the reason you gave and the preference stands there completely untouched, that
is informative. Something else was doing the work, and it is still doing it.

Nothing here calls for distrusting yourself in general, which is paralysing and
unearned. It calls for holding a stated reason more loosely than the certainty
attached to it, and for noticing when an argument you relied on falls over and
changes nothing.

## What experience gives back

In familiar country, experience approaches free accuracy. Someone who has spent
years among a particular kind of problem sees the answer before they can say
why, and their inability to explain it does not make it worse. Pattern
recognition of that sort is real and it is earned, and asking such a person to
justify every step of it wastes what they have.

In unfamiliar country the same machinery runs at the same speed and produces
the same feeling of knowing with none of the substance behind it. The feeling
does not carry a marker for how far it has been stretched. It feels the same
either way, which is the whole problem.

The position that catches people out is the one that resembles familiar country
without being it. Enough surface likeness to summon the pattern, enough
difference underneath to make the pattern wrong. Recognition is a strong signal
and silent about its own limits, so the question worth asking is what exactly
is being recognised, and whether that likeness is the part that matters.

## Hindsight and the memory of a moment

Once you know how something turned out, the state of not knowing is gone and
cannot be recovered by trying. The past self who could not see what is now
obvious looks slow, and the outcome that was one possibility among several
looks like it was always coming. Every retrospective runs uphill against this,
including the private ones.

The damage shows in what gets learned. Judging a past choice by how it turned
out rewards and blames the person for everything they did not control. Careful
thinking that ran into bad luck gets remembered as a mistake. Careless thinking
that got lucky gets remembered as instinct, and repeated. Both lessons are
false and both stick.

The only real defence is a note made before the outcome is known, saying what
was expected and how sure the person was. Written afterwards it is
contaminated. Written at the time it is dull and slightly embarrassing to
reread, which is much of what makes it useful.

## Saying it to someone

Explaining a question to another person changes the question. Something that
sat comfortably as a feeling has to become sentences, and sentences have to be
in an order, and choosing the order forces admissions that were being avoided.
Much of the benefit arrives before the listener has said anything at all.

Vagueness survives indefinitely inside a head and dies on the way out of a
mouth. The listener does not need any expertise in the subject. They need
patience and enough presence to notice when a sentence has gone soft, and the
willingness to say so rather than nod it along.

There is a case for a listener with less knowledge rather than more. An expert
answers, and the answer arrives before the question has finished forming, which
ends the useful part early. Someone who cannot answer has nothing to do but
make you keep going, and that is where the work happens.

## Getting better at it

Skill grows on feedback, and decisions deliver feedback poorly. It comes back
slow, mixed with noise from everything else that was happening, and it concerns
a world that has moved since. The cases anyone accumulates are few, they are
the ones that person happened to live through, and the paths not taken report
nothing.

Given that, improvement comes mostly from being specific in advance. Saying
beforehand what you expect to happen and how confident you are gives surprise
somewhere to land. A vague expectation cannot be disappointed, and an
expectation that cannot be disappointed teaches nothing, however many times it
is tested.

Most people who improve do so by comparing what they thought would happen
against what did, without ceremony. The habit is small and boring and compounds
over years, which is why it is easy to recommend and rare to observe.

## What can be handed over

A person helping with someone else's question can lend attention, patience,
distance from the thing, and the willingness to say plainly what everyone
involved has been circling. That is a genuine contribution and often scarce.

What cannot be lent is the living with it afterwards. The person who asked
keeps the consequences and keeps the responsibility for having chosen, and no
amount of help transfers either. That asymmetry ought to shape the help. The
useful kind leaves someone more able to stand behind their own choice. The
flattering kind leaves them more dependent on whoever helped, which feels
better at the time for both parties.

A conclusion that someone accepts without understanding is borrowed, and
borrowed conclusions do not survive contact with the first person who pushes
back on them. The work is finished when the person holding the question can
hold it themselves.
