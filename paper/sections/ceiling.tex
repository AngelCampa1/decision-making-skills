\section{The Ceiling, and Nine Templates That Did Not Clear It}
\label{sec:ceiling}

This section reports a failure. The study above runs on a 1.7B model and is
formally scoped to it (\Cref{sec:arenas}). The obvious next move is to replicate
on a larger model, and we could not, because the corpus has no room on one. We
tried nine times to build room and failed nine times. What the failures produced
is worth more than the corpus would have been.

One thing to hold while reading it. The screen below is published beside the
study's records as a committed artifact,
\pth{nvbuild-ceiling-screen.json}, and it holds \NUM{\screenModels}
models. The harness registers eleven, eight
returned inside the screen's budget, and one of those eight is recorded in the
notebook entry and missing from the file. We report the file's count here and
leave the artifact alone, because a record that gets edited to match a paper is
not a record. The nine-template work after it ran as a probe through sub-agents
rather than through the checkpointed runner, so those passes wrote no records
and their figures are typed here from the dated notebook entries that recorded
them. The admission-rule subsection is the exception: it turns the criteria on
our own corpus, so its skew and informedness figures come from the five-arm
study's committed records and are typed only because \texttt{de figures} emits
no per-template skew macro. Each
prediction was registered before its probe and the entries say so, which is what
the record here rests on. It is a weaker footing than the five-arm study's and
we would rather name it than have it inferred.

\subsection{Every hosted model we measured solves the corpus}

Screened with an empty prompt on \NUM{\screenItems} items each, every model in
the artifact answers this corpus at or near ceiling: \NUM{\screenAtCeiling} of
\NUM{\screenModels} at \NUM{\screenBest} and the weakest at
\NUM{\screenWorst}, against \NUM{\accUnseenOff} for the study's local target.
Accuracy there is over answered items and not asked ones, and
\NUM{\screenUnanswered} call across the whole screen returned nothing, so one
of the perfect scores is over one item fewer.
Three registered models did not return inside the screen's budget and are
recorded as unmeasured, never assumed; they are larger than models already
answering at \NUM{\screenBest}, so the expectation is clear and it is an
expectation.

A five-arm study needs headroom for an arm to move into. At \NUM{\screenWorst} there is not
enough for any effect this design could detect, so no verdict-bearing venue can
host this study as the corpus stands.

\subsection{Nine templates, and how each one failed}

We authored nine harder templates in two days, in distinct domains, each built
around a rule the model has to select before it can apply: a precedence between
two thresholds, chosen by a categorical variable stated elsewhere in the item.

Six of the nine ceiling on a 30B model. The registered prediction was that a set
of six selection templates would land it between 0.60 and 0.75; the set landed
at 0.949, and five of the six sit at or beside 1.000.

One template held the model off the ceiling, reading 0.722 on the eighteen
items of its first pass. It was the template the prediction generalised from,
and it turned out to be one-sided rather than hard. The bias was then measured
three times on this \emph{item type} rather than on that template alone, at
0.629 on 35 items of it, 0.583 on 24, and 0.625 on 24 items of a reversed
variant built to test it. Each reaches its number with sensitivity 1.000
against specificity of 0.235, 0.167 and 0.250: it answers one option on about
nine items in ten, in a set that wants it on half. That is an
informedness of 0.167 to 0.250, and on a balanced key accuracy is pinned at
$(J + 1)/2$, which is exactly the two balanced measurements, 0.583 and 0.625.
The third ran on a realised 18/17 split, where the identity gives 0.618 against
the 0.629 observed. A nudge toward the other option would move both error rates
and leave $J$ where it is, so it would gain nothing. An earlier draft said it
would gain roughly thirty points, which is worth zero on a balanced key.
\texttt{docs/PROTOCOL.md} carried the same sentence and now records its
retraction. The template is not hard and it is not biased in a way any arm could
exploit. It is barely discriminating, which is the reason to retire it.

Two more templates were built to diagnose that one, and both diagnoses were
wrong. Adding two high-strength money facts to a ceilinged template, to invite
weighing cost instead of applying the rule, changed nothing: 23 of 23 against a
control's 23 of 23. Reversing which option is named first, on the hypothesis
that the bias was positional, moved accuracy by 0.042 against a registered
threshold of 0.10.

\subsection{Three registered predictions, all falsified}

Each was written before its run and each stayed in the record saying so.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Six further selection templates would land a 30B between 0.60 and 0.75
    as a set. They landed at 0.949.
  \item An arm that gains accuracy on the one-sided template would gain it by
    moving skew. No arm gained, so the premise never occurred: over 36 items and
    three arms, no skill and the placebo read 0.694 and 0.697, and the seed
    skill read 0.600.
  \item Reversing the option order would move accuracy by more than 0.10. It
    moved 0.042, and the bias is therefore semantic and still unexplained.
\end{enumerate}

The third arm of prediction 2 is worth reporting on its own, because it is the
only case in this project where a skill visibly traded one property for another.
The seed skill cut skew from $+0.306$ to $+0.171$ and cut informedness from
0.389 to 0.190 at the same time: a smaller lean bought with less discrimination,
and a net loss of 0.094 accuracy. Every interval crosses zero at 36 items, which
is the other thing that run showed. It was sized against an expected
thirty-point gap and cannot see a ten-point one.

\subsection{The admission rule this bought}

``Make the corpus harder'' is not a specification, and following it produced
nine templates that were either easy or one-sided. Three criteria replace it,
all computable from the control-arm records a calibration run already produces:

\begin{itemize}
  \item accuracy below the ceiling, so an arm has somewhere to move;
  \item skew near zero, so the model is answering from the item;
  \item informedness below one, so discrimination is left to gain.
\end{itemize}

Seven published templates and six of the nine hard ones have signal and no
room, on a large model. The one-sided ones have room and no signal.

\textbf{And the three criteria do not close the case, which we found by turning
them on our own corpus.} \texttt{rel-009-flight-rebook} passes all three on the
empty-prompt arm: reported accuracy
\NUM{\accUnseenRelZeroZeroNineFlightRebookOff},
inside the band and far below any ceiling; skew of $+0.019$, nearer zero than
every template in the corpus but the two that read exactly $0.000$; and
informedness of $+0.028$, which is certainly below one. It is also one of the three templates \Cref{sec:signal}
marks as measuring nothing. A coin has zero skew and zero informedness too. The
third criterion bounds discrimination above and not below, and a lower bound is
the obvious repair, but we have not run a corpus against one and will not
present an untested rule as a result. What this section delivers is a criterion
set that is necessary, demonstrably not sufficient, and better than the accuracy
band it replaces.

A fourth criterion is untested and we state it as a hypothesis rather than a
rule: both options should be acts, so neither is the default a model reaches for
under uncertainty. We tested the nearest version of it and it did not hold.
Signed toward the act on the 1.7B, six act-or-wait templates average $+0.097$,
carried almost entirely by one template at $+0.464$; dropping it leaves the rest
at $+0.024$. Three two-act templates average absolute skew of 0.172, which is
higher rather than lower.

Six and three is nine, and the corpus has ten.
\texttt{rel-005-security-patch} is in neither group: its options are
\texttt{patch\_immediately} and \texttt{schedule\_patch}, which are both acts
under one reading and an act against a deferral under the other, and we could
not classify it without choosing the answer. It carries the second-largest skew
magnitude in the corpus at $-0.400$, behind the $+0.464$ quoted just above. Putting it in the first group takes $+0.097$
to $+0.026$; putting it in the second takes 0.172 to 0.229. The direction of the
comparison survives either placement and the two averages do not, which is the
honest summary of a nine-template test on a ten-template corpus. The action-bias
explanation is refuted on our data and the underlying cause is open.

\subsection{What would actually unblock this}

We record the options instead of pretending we have chosen. A corpus of more than
two options per item removes the specific confound this section is about, at the
cost of a harder scoring path and a weaker pairing. A multi-step task, where the
model must carry a result forward, changes what is being measured rather than
how hard it is. And a genuinely hard rule-selection template may exist and we
may simply have failed to write one, which the nine failures make less likely
without ruling out.

What we will not do is tune a template until a model finds it hard. That
procedure produces a corpus fitted to one model's weaknesses, and the arena rule
that scopes this study exists to stop exactly that reading.
