one-sided sign-flip cannot return below $2^{-k}$, because the observed sign

own tail. Equality holds only when it also maximises the statistic. The unseen
set has \NUM{\templatesUnseen} templates, so its floor is \NUM{\floorUnseen},
above the family's $\alpha = \NUM{\studyAlpha}$ before Holm and further above
Holm's tightest step of \NUM{\mdeAlpha} after. \emph{No outcome of the three
unseen comparisons could have been significant.} The seen set has
\NUM{\templatesSeen} templates and a design floor of \NUM{\floorSeen}, which
leaves room. The realised floor is higher, because a template that nets zero
drops out of the test: the three seen comparisons ran on
\NUM{\clustersSeenSkillopt}, \NUM{\clustersSeenGepa} and
\NUM{\clustersSeenOn} live clusters, floors of
\NUM{\clusteredFloorSeenSkillopt} for the first two and
\NUM{\clusteredFloorSeenOn} for the third, and the last is already above
Holm's tightest $\alpha = \NUM{\mdeAlpha}$.

This is a property of the design, not of the data. It was computable when the
sets were drawn, and we did not compute it. \Cref{sec:limitations} carries
what that costs the registered predictions, and the companion check, a minimum
detectable effect recomputed at the design's own clustering, sits beside it
there.

\subsection{An answer the scorer refused, and where the refusals fell}
\label{sec:parsefail}

Every arm's unreadable answers are scored wrong. That is the study's
convention and it is defensible, but it makes accuracy a sum of decision
quality and format compliance, and on this record part of the second is the
scorer's.

\texttt{/no\_think} as a switch for its thinking mode. On
\NUM{\controlTokenTotal} of the \NUM{\studyReadings} arm readings the model

\subsection{What accuracy hides}
\label{sec:signal}

Everything in this subsection is recomputed from the records above. No model
was called. The records carry the answer key and the model's answer on every
row, so the rate the model chose an option can be compared against the rate
the items wanted it.

\paragraph{The measure.} Accuracy on a two-option key adds together two
different quantities: how well a model tells the cases apart, and how often it
picks one option whatever the case. Write accuracy as
$\pi \cdot \mathrm{sens} + (1 - \pi) \cdot \mathrm{spec}$ for a base rate
$\pi$. At $\pi = 0.5$ that collapses to $(J + 1)/2$, a function of
informedness alone, where informedness is Youden's
$J = \mathrm{sensitivity} + \mathrm{specificity} - 1$: zero for any
constant-answer policy at any base rate, unmoved by a pure shift in
preference, and symmetric in which option is called positive. Every template
in this corpus is balanced by construction, and the identity holds in our
records to the fourth decimal in every cell where no answer went unread. So on
this corpus response bias contributes nothing to accuracy, and the case for
reporting $J$ rests on two other things it exposes: which templates carry any
discrimination at all, and what happens when parse failure breaks the
identity by landing unevenly on the key (\Cref{sec:parsefail}). \emph{Skew},
the model's rate for an option minus the rate the items wanted it, names the
bias half directly; we report the mean of its absolute value over templates.

We compute both per template and average over templates, so no template's item
count decides the answer. Rows the model did not parse are excluded, a
limitation \Cref{sec:limitations} carries. A template whose key holds a single
answer class has no informedness at all, and the implementation refuses to
return a value for it: recording an undefined cell as zero would put it into a
mean as though it were a measurement of no discrimination.

\subsection{What the engines actually produced}
\label{sec:whatengines}

The scores above are the answer to the registered question. What the winners
contain is a separate finding and does not depend on a score.

\paragraph{Provenance, first.} The two winners' bodies were written into a
results directory our \texttt{.gitignore} excludes, were never committed, and
no longer exist (\Cref{sec:limitations}). The descriptions of their contents
below are typed from the dated notebook entries that recorded them at the
time, and a reader should weigh them as that. The figures describing how the
winners then \emph{scored} are macros from the study's committed records and
carry the same provenance as every other number in this section.

\textbf{Both of the winners we tested had written training-item constants into
the skill body, and both engines accepted them on evidence too thin to have
noticed.}

GEPA's winner carries a rules table asserting renewal at 61\% utilisation and
capacity headroom at 37\%, and a worked example built on the numbers 2818 and
2032. Every one of those is a value from a single training item. The catch is
that \texttt{utilisation\_floor} is drawn fresh per item from the integer
range \NUM{\rangeRelZeroZeroEightContractRenewUtilisationfloorLow} to
\NUM{\rangeRelZeroZeroEightContractRenewUtilisationfloorHigh} and stated in
each item's Background, so a body carrying 61\% asserts a threshold its own
prompt contradicts on almost every item it meets.

Whether that cost anything is a question this split cannot answer. Both
constants belong to \texttt{rel-008-contract-renew} and
\texttt{rel-007-capacity-scale}, both are training templates, and neither is
in the unseen set. On the two templates concerned GEPA reads
\NUM{\accSeenRelZeroZeroEightContractRenewGepa} and
\NUM{\accSeenRelZeroZeroSevenCapacityScaleGepa} against an empty prompt's
\NUM{\accSeenRelZeroZeroEightContractRenewOff} and
\NUM{\accSeenRelZeroZeroSevenCapacityScaleOff}, at or above it. Its
registered unseen deficit is elsewhere, on \texttt{rel-001-vendor-outage},
which none of the constants touch and which \Cref{sec:parsefail} attributes
to the scorer.

SkillOpt is the harder case. On
\texttt{rel-008-contract-renew}, the one template it lifted a constant from,
it reads \NUM{\accSeenRelZeroZeroEightContractRenewSkillopt} against
\NUM{\accSeenRelZeroZeroEightContractRenewOff} for an empty prompt, and that
template carries \NUM{\netSeenRelZeroZeroEightContractRenewSkillopt} of the
net items behind \NUM{\effectSeenSkillopt}. That net is a signed sum over
templates, not a partition, so this is a share of the total and not of the
positives: \texttt{rel-005-security-patch} carries
\NUM{\netSeenRelZeroZeroFiveSecurityPatchSkillopt} with no constant lifted
from it, and the other five templates sum against both. What is left is that
the second largest contribution to the study's largest gain sits on the one
template its winner memorised a threshold for. One template, one engine, an
unregistered comparison.

SkillOpt's winner carries 63\% for \texttt{rel-008-contract-renew}, written
into the body with the worked arithmetic $213/300$ beside it. Two engines,
one training pool, two different constants for one rule. GEPA also lifted from
\texttt{rel-007-capacity-scale}; SkillOpt did not, and 63 could not have come
from that template, whose own variable is drawn from
\NUM{\rangeRelZeroZeroSevenCapacityScaleHeadroompctLow} to
\NUM{\rangeRelZeroZeroSevenCapacityScaleHeadroompctHigh}.

How far the pair generalises is bounded by what survives. Six frozen winners
were on disk across this project's searches when we counted; the notebook
records three carrying constants lifted from single training items and three
carrying none, it names three of the six, and the bodies behind all six are
gone, so nobody can now re-derive that split, ourselves included. On that
record, memorisation is a common outcome of an accept-if-strictly-better
ratchet, and not a certain one. Neither engine was asked to memorise.

\paragraph{What each engine accepted its winner on.} SkillOpt scored all
nine of its candidates on all 21 validation items: seed 18, winner 21, one
unreplicated comparison with no interval on either side. GEPA's record for
the tested run is thinner. Our lineage writes a candidate once, with the
first score it received, and GEPA scores a new candidate first on a
reflection minibatch of three; so the lineage carries generation zero on 21
items and every later candidate on three, and \texttt{winner.json}, which
falls back to the lineage score when the engine chose, closes at $1.000$ over
$n = 3$ with \texttt{winner\_source = engine}. That run's own search log is
gone, so its validation-pool aggregate cannot be recovered.

A later GEPA search, on a different template set and run under the same
adapter, committed its search log in full and settles what the three-item
figure was. At the iteration that produced its frozen winner, the log
records a validation aggregate of $20/21 = 0.952$, individual scores for
every one of the 21 items, and names that candidate the best program on the
valset; the proposal text at that iteration is byte-identical to the run's
\texttt{winner.md} and hashes to the \texttt{candidate\_sha} in its
\texttt{winner.json}, which itself closes at \texttt{score 1.0} over
\texttt{n\_items 3}, the same two fields the tested run's file carries.
\textbf{GEPA evaluates and selects its winner on the full validation pool;
\texttt{n\_items 3} is our own adapter recording the first score a candidate
was seen on, which for GEPA is the reflection minibatch, and not the
engine's acceptance evidence.} The mechanism runs in the same adapter that
produced the tested run's lineage, so the three-item figure this paper
reports for that run is the same artifact and not a measure of what GEPA
accepted its winner on. What stays unrecoverable is that run's own
aggregate, because its search log does not exist: GEPA's $1.000$ over three
and its seed's $0.762$ over 21 remain not one measurement and cannot be
differenced. What the settled mechanism moves is where the finding lands:
not a criticism of GEPA's acceptance rule, which evaluates on the full pool
as documented, but a defect in our own instrumentation of someone else's
optimiser, caught only because a later run kept the engine's own search log
rather than our adapter's summary of it. The document the search handed back
had memorised regardless.
