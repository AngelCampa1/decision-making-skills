\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Markdown ``skills'' are a real distribution channel. The Agent Skills standard
fixes a \texttt{SKILL.md} format and a shared \texttt{.agents/skills/} discovery
path, and its own client showcase listed forty-six adopting products in August
2026, among them Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, VS Code, Gemini CLI
and Amp~\citep{agentskills2025}. These files are installed and running inside
coding agents today. Almost none of them carry evidence that they help.

A second layer has grown on top of the first. Engines now rewrite skill files
automatically: GEPA proposes whole-prompt revisions from reflection over failed
rollouts~\citep{gepa2026}, and Microsoft's SkillOpt makes bounded edits to a
skill body and keeps any edit that scores better~\citep{skillopt2026}. Both
accept an edit on a single uncontrolled score improvement, hundreds of times in
a run.

That acceptance rule is the problem this paper is about. A ratchet that keeps
every improvement it observes performs an enormous number of implicit
comparisons, and reports none of them. It has no notion of a difference that
could have arisen by chance, so it cannot distinguish a skill that got better
from a skill that got luckier. The engines' own evaluations inherit the gap:
single-run point estimates on fixed splits, with no repeats, no intervals, no
significance tests, and no control for what a document-shaped file does when its
content says nothing at all. Where prompt optimisation has been audited that way
in a neighbouring setting, 49\% of 72 optimisation runs scored below
zero-shot~\citep{promptcoinflip2026}.

The literature that these engines are measured against does not agree with
itself either. SkillsBench reports that making a skill \emph{available} is worth
$+18$ to $+36$ percentage points, while the \emph{granularity of its prose} is
worth $+0.7$ points on one model and $-6.7$ on the other, both with intervals
crossing zero~\citep{skillsbench2026}. SkillOpt reports $+23.5$ points from
optimising exactly that prose, on GPT-5.5 in direct chat, with $+24.8$ inside
Codex and $+19.1$ inside Claude Code~\citep{skillopt2026}. Both cannot be right
in the general case, and neither headline travels with the statistic that would
settle it.

\paragraph{What we did.}
We held the model fixed, evolved a decision-making skill with each engine
against a training split, froze both winners by content hash, and then minted a
fresh holdout the engines had never seen. Five arms ran over that holdout: no
skill, the hand-written seed skill, a placebo matched to that skill on word
count and structure, and the two evolved winners, whose lengths the engines
chose and which the placebo therefore does not match
(\Cref{sec:length-confound}). Every comparison is
McNemar's exact test against the placebo, paired by item, with Holm correction
across a family of \NUM{\familySize} registered before the first call. The prediction was
committed to git before any data existed, and the run's provenance gate refuses
a result whose prediction cannot be shown by ancestry to predate it.

\paragraph{What we found.}
No arm rejects, on either item set, after correction. The best showing is
SkillOpt's winner at $\NUM{\effectSeenSkillopt}$ on the scenarios it trained
against, which does not survive multiplicity. On scenarios neither engine saw,
GEPA's winner falls furthest below an empty prompt, and the skill we wrote falls
below it too.
Both winners had written training-item constants into their own bodies, and each
asserted a different threshold for the same rule on a variable the corpus draws
fresh for every item. GEPA accepted its winner on three items, by its own
acceptance rule, and never scored it on the validation pool at all.

Then a null is only worth reading if the study could have returned something
else, so we checked, and it could not have. Our items come from templates and
our own limitations section says the template is the independent unit. Under
that unit the unseen set is three clusters, a one-sided test over three clusters
cannot return a $p$ below \NUM{\floorUnseen}, and all three of our registered
unseen comparisons were therefore unfalsifiable at any outcome. The seen set
survives the same check but its headline does not: SkillOpt's
$p = \NUM{\pSeenSkillopt}$ becomes \NUM{\clusteredSeenSkillopt} once templates
rather than items are resampled. The pre-registration had also computed a
minimum detectable effect and put it at \NUM{\mdeUnseen} on the unseen set,
which is already above anything the study found; recomputed at the clustering
our protocol specifies it is \NUM{\mdeClusteredUnseen}. Every one of those
numbers was available from the registration before a single call was made, and
none of them reached the paper until now. We think that is the part worth
carrying to the next study, and we have put it in front of the null rather than
in a caveat behind it.

We also report a result about the measurement, not the engines. A pooled
accuracy says nothing about which templates in a corpus discriminate, and the
metric this study reports, which scores an unreadable answer as wrong, does not
rank them either: our best-discriminating template reports below one of our
worst. Computing discrimination per template left the study's conclusion
standing and put \NUM{\lowSignalTemplates} of our \NUM{\studyTemplates}
templates below $J = \NUM{\lowSignalThreshold}$ in every arm.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
% Each of these stands whether the engines succeeded or failed. That is
% deliberate: a contribution contingent on a positive result is a bet.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The first placebo-controlled evaluation of automated skill
    optimisation we are aware of.} A skill that beats an empty prompt but not a
    document of matched length and structure whose content says nothing has
    demonstrated a length effect, and no engine in this space reports the
    comparison that would tell the two apart. Ours is matched to the seed skill
    and not to the evolved winners, which \Cref{sec:length-confound} reports as
    a limit on what the arm controls.
  \item \textbf{An A/A control, reported.} The control arm was scored a second
    time into a separate checkpoint, and returned \NUM{\aaPairs} of
    \NUM{\aaPairs} items identical. The venue's determinism is a measurement
    rather than an assumption, and it bounds the drift the block ordering
    exposes the arms to over the stretch the repeat covers.
  \item \textbf{The arithmetic showing our own registered design was
    undecidable, computed from the registration rather than from the outcome.}
    Three of six primaries could not have rejected at any result, because a
    one-sided test over \NUM{\templatesUnseen} template clusters has a
    $p$-floor of \NUM{\floorUnseen}; and the effect the design could detect was
    larger than the effect it found before the run began. Both checks are two
    lines of arithmetic over a registration, both are absent from every skill
    optimisation paper we read, and either would have stopped this study.
  \item \textbf{A discrimination measure reported beside accuracy}, applied to
    our own published records, showing that a
    \NUM{\totalItems}-item run carried the discriminative signal of
    \NUM{\signalItems} (\Cref{sec:signal}). We argue the failure generalises to
    the two-option evaluations this literature is built on.
  \item \textbf{A negative result about corpus construction}, reported at the
    same prominence as the rest: no verdict-bearing replication of this study
    can be built on this corpus, nine attempts to build a harder one failed, and
    the way they failed produced an admission rule (\Cref{sec:ceiling}).
  \item \textbf{Methodological apparatus this literature lacks}, all of it open:
    hash-locked pre-registration verified by git ancestry, paired exact tests
    reported beside cluster sign-flip tests and bootstrap intervals over
    templates, Holm correction across a registered family, and a generator that
    computes ground truth from template rules rather than authoring it.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope, stated once and up front.}
This is one target model, one corpus, one harness. The target is a 1.7B
parameter local model, chosen because every larger model we measured solves
the corpus with an empty prompt (\Cref{sec:ceiling}). The repository's own arena
rule declines to issue a verdict from a run at that tier, and we honour it: the
claim here is that on this model, under these controls, neither engine's output
beat a placebo. Whether it would on a frontier model is open, and
\Cref{sec:ceiling} is an account of why we could not answer it.
