\subsection{Skills as an intervention, and a live disagreement}

Two 2026 results on the same object point in opposite directions.
SkillsBench~\citep{skillsbench2026} runs a 30-task domain-balanced subset across
two models, six skill conditions and five trials, and separates two questions:
whether a skill is \emph{available} to the agent, and how its prose is
\emph{written}. Availability is worth $+18.0$ to $+36.0$ percentage points, the
union of the two models' ranges. Granularity of the prose is worth $+0.7$ points
on one model and $-6.7$ on the other, with intervals crossing zero. Read on its
own, the finding is that the retrieval matters and the writing does not.

SkillOpt~\citep{skillopt2026} reports $+23.5$ points from bounded edits to
exactly that prose. That figure is one harness: GPT-5.5 in direct chat. The same
sentence gives $+24.8$ inside Codex and $+19.1$ inside Claude Code, and quoting
the first without the other two is the error that
\citet{harnessdisclosure2026} exists to name.

Both cannot hold in the general case, and the disagreement is not obviously
resolvable from either paper: SkillOpt's headline travels without an interval,
and SkillsBench's crosses zero. Our design was built to adjudicate it, and this study is a
partial answer at one model scale.

\paragraph{On SkillOpt specifically.}
Its method is sound in outline and its harness choice is the right one: it
evaluates inside the agent tools people actually run, which is more than most of
this literature does. The criticism here is narrow, and narrower than an
earlier draft made it. Its abstract reports no confidence interval, no
significance test and no correction for the many implicit comparisons its
accept-if-strictly-better ratchet performs, and we did not read the full paper,
so what we can say is that the headline figure travels without them and not
that the paper contains none. We reproduce its
engine faithfully and test its output, rather than reanalysing its numbers, and
where a claim about the paper's contents rests on a section we have not read
first-hand we leave it out of this paper instead of repeating it.

\paragraph{GEPA.}
GEPA~\citep{gepa2026} performs reflective whole-prompt evolution with a Pareto
frontier over rollouts, and reports large gains over reinforcement-learning
baselines at a fraction of the rollout budget. Its acceptance criterion has the
same shape: a candidate enters the frontier on observed score. We use it as
distributed, through its documented adapter interface, with an
OpenAI-compatible reflection model.

\subsection{Why a placebo arm is the load-bearing control}

The AGENTS.md impact study~\citep{agentsmd2026} and the skill-authoring
literature~\citep{authoringskills2026} both report that adding an instruction
artifact helps. Neither compares against an artifact of matched length and
structure whose content says nothing. Without that comparison, ``the skill
helped'' and ``a document-shaped block of tokens in the system prompt helped''
are the same observation.

Our placebo is generated to match the shipped skill on word count and on
structural shape, and it ships in the repository beside the skill so the
comparison can be re-run. \Cref{sec:results} reports it as a first-class arm,
and every arm in the registered family is tested against it. It matches the shipped skill and not
the evolved winners, and \Cref{sec:length-confound} gives the sizes.

\subsection{Harness variance, and what a verdict is worth}

\citet{harnessdisclosure2026} run three frontier models across three harness
configurations on a stratified subset of SWE-bench Verified and report an
aggregate harness-to-model variance ratio of $7.8\times$, with the ranking of
models reversing in six of nine comparisons. One $3\times3$ design on one task
distribution: we read the direction as transferring and the ratio as not.
\citet{harnessbench2026} reach the qualitative half of the same conclusion over
5{,}194 trajectories on 106 sandboxed tasks, where completion, process quality
and failure behaviour all vary substantially across model-harness pairings. A
result measured inside one scaffold is a claim about that scaffold as much as
about the model, and almost nobody reports the scaffold in enough detail to
check. We give a full disclosure in \Cref{sec:appendix-harness}, under our own
seven-heading scheme, against the requirement
\citet{harnessdisclosure2026} sets, and we do not assume our result transfers to a
different agent harness.

Prompting Inversion~\citep{promptinginversion2025} sharpens this for our case
specifically: scaffolding tuned against a weak model can become a handicap on a
strong one. Our engines optimised against a 1.7B model. That is a reason to
expect the transfer question to have its own answer, and a reason we decline to
extrapolate from ours.

\subsection{Statistics for evaluations at this scale}

\citet{miller2024errorbars} popularised CLT-based standard errors for
evaluations. \citet{noclt2025} shows the normal approximation is unreliable
below a few hundred effectively independent datapoints, which is where a
clustered design at our item count sits. We use McNemar's exact
test~\citep{mcnemar1947note} for paired binary outcomes and a cluster bootstrap
over templates for intervals, and we report the discordant split alongside every
$p$-value.

The multiplicity problem in this neighbourhood is larger than the reported
comparisons suggest, because the ratchet inside each engine performs comparisons
that never reach a table. We cannot correct for those. We correct for ours,
across a family of three fixed before the first call, with Holm's step-down
procedure. Benjamini--Hochberg~\citep{benjamini1995fdr} is implemented in the
same module and controls the false discovery rate rather than the family-wise
error rate; with three registered comparisons and no rejection at either
criterion, the choice does not change the result.
