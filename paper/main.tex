\begin{abstract}
Two open-source engines, GEPA and Microsoft's SkillOpt, automatically rewrite
the markdown ``skill'' files that agent tools load, and both accept an edit on a
single uncontrolled score improvement. We evolved a decision-making skill with
each engine, then tested five arms against each other on a holdout minted after
both winners were frozen: no skill, the hand-written seed skill, a token- and
structure-matched placebo, and the two evolved winners. Comparisons are McNemar
exact against the placebo with Holm correction across a pre-registered family of
three, on \NUM{\totalItems} items.

\textbf{No arm rejects on either item set.} The best showing is SkillOpt at
$+\NUM{\effectSeenSkillopt}$ on trained scenarios, raw $p = \NUM{\pSeenSkillopt}$,
which Holm takes to \NUM{\qSeenSkillopt}. On scenarios neither engine saw,
GEPA's winner scores \NUM{\accUnseenGepa} against \NUM{\accUnseenOff} for an
empty prompt: worse than no skill at all. An A/A control returned
\NUM{\aaPairs} of \NUM{\aaPairs} items identical, so every difference between
arms is one the prompts caused. Both winners had written the answer key into
their own bodies.

We also report a measurement result about the instrument. Accuracy on a
two-option key adds discrimination to response bias and reports only their sum.
Decomposing it leaves the headline standing and shows that \NUM{\lowSignalTemplates}
of \NUM{\studyTemplates} templates carry no discriminative signal at all, so a
\NUM{\totalItems}-item run carries the signal of \NUM{\signalItems}. We argue
this failure mode is generic to the two-option evaluations this literature is
built on.
\end{abstract}
